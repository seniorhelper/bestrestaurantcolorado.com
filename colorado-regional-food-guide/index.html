Colorado's regional foods, by town and by month

Colorado's signature foods carry the names of the towns that grow them. Here is where to find each one, when it peaks, and how to build a trip around the harvest.

Eat Colorado by going to where it grows

Colorado's best-known foods are tied to towns, not to the state as a whole. A Palisade peach, an Olathe ear of corn, a Rocky Ford melon and a Pueblo chile each carry the name of a specific place, and each has a short season, mostly in late summer. That makes them a travel plan as much as a menu: the surest way to taste them at their best is to be near where they grow, in the weeks they are picked.

This guide is organized that way, by region and by month, with the festivals that celebrate each crop. The restaurants that cook with these ingredients are spread across the state, and many of the kitchens on our award register put them on the menu in season. For Denver's own dishes, green chile included, see what to eat in Denver on our sister site.

Why the produce tastes the way it does

The short version is altitude and climate. Colorado's farm country sees hot, bright days and cool nights, and the swing between them helps fruit and vegetables build sugar. Much of it is also irrigated high desert, which is why a valley like Palisade's grows peaches at all. Growers and the state's agriculture department promote local produce under the Colorado Proud label, which is a quick way to spot in-state food at markets and grocery stores.

The four crops and their towns

Western Slope · late June to early October

Palisade peaches

Orchards east of Grand Junction, watered by river-fed ditches that settlers began digging in the late nineteenth century. Picking starts around the end of June and tails off in early autumn, with midsummer the sweet spot. The town throws its Peach Festival every August at Riverbend Park.

Western Slope · August

Olathe sweet corn

Grown around Olathe, a small Uncompahgre Valley town just north of Montrose, not far from Black Canyon of the Gunnison. Its corn festival runs every August. Sweet corn loses sugar fast after harvest, so buying near the field genuinely matters.

Arkansas Valley · mid to late summer

Rocky Ford melons

Rocky Ford, a small Arkansas Valley town east of Pueblo, has grown melons commercially since 1887 and bills itself as the world's sweet melon capital. Its school sports teams are named the Meloneers, a fair measure of local pride.

Pueblo County · August to October

Pueblo chiles

A mirasol-type pepper, named for the way it grows pointing toward the sun, grown around Pueblo since at least 1903. Fire-roasting is the classic treatment, and the Pueblo Chile and Frijoles Festival on Union Avenue each September is the big event of the season.

Add two more. Potatoes from the high, flat San Luis Valley, a major U.S. potato region, are on menus all over the state. And wine: Palisade's vineyards anchor Colorado's industry, alongside the Grand Valley and West Elks American Viticultural Areas.

Turning it into a trip

September is when the calendars line up: late-season peaches in the Grand Valley, Pueblo's roasters running flat out, and high-country towns calm after summer but still open before the October lull. A loop from Denver south to Pueblo, west over the mountains and back along I-70 through the Grand Valley takes in all of it. Buying, carrying and cooking it

  • Buy near the source. Roadside stands in Palisade, Olathe and around Pueblo sell the same day's harvest. Ask when it was picked.
  • Bring a cooler. Mountain sun and long drives are hard on fruit and corn. A cooler in the car keeps a peach from turning into jam before you get home.
  • Chile by the bag, roasted on site. Roasters at Pueblo stands and the festival will roast chiles while you wait. Roasted chiles freeze well, which is why people buy them in quantity.
  • Cooking at altitude. If you are cooking in a mountain rental, remember that water boils cooler the higher you go; Colorado State University Extension's altitude tables show roughly nine degrees lost by 5,000 feet and fourteen by 7,500. Corn and anything simmered will take a little longer.
  • On a restaurant menu. In season, many of the state's recognized kitchens name the farm or town on the menu. If they do not, ask; in late summer the answer is often impressively local.
Questions

Common questions

What foods is Colorado known for?

Colorado's signature produce is tied to specific towns: Palisade peaches, Olathe sweet corn, Rocky Ford melons and Pueblo chiles, plus San Luis Valley potatoes and Western Slope wine. Most of them peak in late summer.

When is Palisade peach season?

Roughly late June into early October, with the peak in July and August. The Palisade Peach Festival is held each August at Riverbend Park in Palisade.

What is a Pueblo chile?

A mirasol-type chile grown around Pueblo, Colorado, named for the way the pods grow pointing toward the sun. Pueblo has grown chiles since at least 1903, and the chiles are usually fire-roasted. The Chile and Frijoles Festival is held in Pueblo each September.

What is the best month for a Colorado food trip?

September. Late peaches, peak Pueblo chile and Arkansas Valley melons overlap, and the mountain towns are quieter before the October shoulder season.
